About this model

This is a simplified, qualitative model of the left ventricle, the arteries and the veins, for normal physiology and exercise. It shows direction and relative size of change, not values for a real person. Taylor, the typical person, starts at HR 70 bpm, EDV 120 mL, ESV 50 mL, SV 70 mL, EF 58%, CO 4.9 L/min and BP 120/80 mmHg, in line with the representative values in the lecture.

  • Drew, Taylor and Elliot are three illustrative 30-year-olds who differ only in fitness. Their numbers are modeled to follow the adaptations covered in Module 3, Lab 5 (resting heart rate lower and stroke volume higher with training, maximal heart rate unchanged, higher maximal output through stroke volume). They are illustrations, not measured data. Resting pressure is set a few mmHg higher in Drew and a few lower in Elliot; trials in people with normal pressure find only small training effects at rest (Cornelissen & Smart 2013), so treat these differences as small and illustrative.
  • Cardiac output equals venous return in the steady state. The model finds the filling pressure where what the veins return equals what the heart pumps.
  • Preload (filling pressure, set by venous return) moves the heart along the end-diastolic pressure-volume curve. Contractility changes the slope of the end-systolic curve. Afterload is the arterial pressure the ventricle must reach to eject. Heart rate sets the time available for filling.
  • BP = cardiac output × resistance. Pulse pressure rises with stroke volume and with faster ejection.
  • The exercise slider moves heart rate, contractility, muscle pump, venoconstriction, breathing and resistance together toward a maximal effort. Maximal heart rate is set to 190 bpm. Stroke volume plateaus in the middle of the range in the typical person (Powers Ch. 9). The resistance curve is shaped so that systolic pressure rises with effort while diastolic stays within about 5 mmHg of its resting value through moderate effort and then falls near maximal effort, the usual normal pattern.
  • The Heavy lift scenario is a brief surge of resistance and heart rate with little change in output. It shows direction only: direct arterial recordings in lifters show far larger peaks, helped by a held breath and compression of the working muscle (MacDougall 1985, 1992), which the model does not reproduce.
  • The reflex switch adds a simple baroreflex: a fall in mean pressure raises heart rate, contractility, venous tone and sympathetic vasoconstriction.
  • The Vessels & BP view splits total resistance into five arteriolar beds in parallel (working muscle, gut and kidney, brain, heart, skin). Each bed's conductance follows radius to the fourth power, and total resistance is one divided by the sum of the bed conductances. Resting shares of cardiac output (muscle about 20%, gut and kidney about half, brain 14%, skin 10%, heart 5%) are typical textbook values. The sensitivities to each cause are tuned by hand so that the default exercise response matches the lecture pattern; they are illustrative, not measured. Extreme slider settings show direction only. Capillary exchange and the renin-angiotensin system are not modeled.
  • Right-heart, lung and valve details are not modeled. Disease states are not part of this version.
